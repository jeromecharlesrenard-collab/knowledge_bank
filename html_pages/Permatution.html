Factorial counts for a round table (6 and 12 people), a rectangular table (12 people), and a two-row jury (12 people) — plus how those counts compare to Roger Penrose's estimate of the odds against the universe's low-entropy start.

Round Table — 6 People

Topics(n−1)!, mirror images
Count
(6−1)! = 5! = 120 arrangements.
Why not 6!
A round table has no fixed starting seat, so rotating everyone by one or more places turns one seating into another that looks identical. Each distinct seating is counted 6 times in the raw 6! = 720 orderings, so dividing by 6 leaves 120.
Mirror images merged
If a clockwise order and its counterclockwise mirror are also treated as the same seating, divide by 2 again: 5!/2 = 60.

Round Table — 12 People

Topics(n−1)!, mirror images
Count
(12−1)! = 11! = 39,916,800, about 39 million arrangements.
Why
Same logic as the 6-person table: no fixed starting seat, so the 12 rotations of each seating count once instead of separately.
Mirror images merged
11!/2 = 19,958,400 arrangements.

Rectangular Table — 12 People

TopicsSix per side, partial symmetry
Setup
12 people, six seats on each of the two long sides.
Seats treated as fixed (each chair distinct)
12! = 479,001,600 — every chair is a distinct, nameable position.
Standard answer: rotation treated as equal
12!/2 = 239,500,800. A rectangle looks the same again only after a 180° turn (unlike a round table, which looks the same after any of 12 rotations), so each arrangement is counted twice rather than 12 times.
Mirror images also merged
12!/4 = 119,750,400, which additionally accounts for flipping the table lengthwise or widthwise.

Jury — 2 Rows, 12 People

TopicsFixed seats, 12!
Count
12! = 479,001,600, about 480 million arrangements.
Why
Jury seats are fixed positions — it makes no difference whether the 12 jurors sit in one row or split across two rows, each of the 12 seats is a distinct, nameable position, so nothing is divided out.

Penrose Number

TopicsEntropy, odds against a low-entropy Big Bang
What it is
Roger Penrose's estimate of the odds that the universe began, purely by chance, in its observed low-entropy state: about 1 in 1010¹²³.
Source
First published in The Emperor's New Mind (1989); revisited in The Road to Reality (2004).
How it is derived
Penrose took the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy of a black hole holding all the mass of the observable universe (about 10¹²³ in natural units) as the maximum possible entropy. The fraction of phase space matching our actual low-entropy starting point is then roughly e−10¹²³, about 10−10¹²³.
Scale
Written out in full, the number has far more digits than there are atoms in the observable universe (about 10⁸⁰).
Why it matters
Penrose used this figure to argue that the Big Bang's special initial conditions need a physical explanation, not just luck — a case that motivated his Weyl Curvature Hypothesis and, later, his Conformal Cyclic Cosmology.
