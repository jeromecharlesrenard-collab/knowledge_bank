Major World Religions

The 12 major religious groups of the world, plus the non-religious, in chronological order. Click the expand icon to read about each one.

Traditional African & Indigenous Religions

OriginPrehistoric

Diverse belief systems passed down orally across Africa, the Americas, Oceania, and elsewhere. Many share belief in a supreme creator, ancestral spirits, and spiritual forces in nature. Rituals, community, and connection to land are central. Some, like Yoruba religion, influenced diaspora faiths such as Santería and Candomblé.

Shinto

OriginPrehistoric

Japan's indigenous religion, with roots in prehistoric times and no founder or central scripture. It centers on kami, spirits present in nature, places, ancestors, and the imperial lineage. It emphasizes ritual purity, shrine worship, and harmony with nature. It coexists closely with Buddhism in Japanese life.

Chinese Folk Religion

OriginAncient

An ancient, decentralized set of practices rooted in Chinese culture for thousands of years. It blends ancestor veneration, worship of local and household gods, and elements of Taoism, Buddhism, and Confucianism. It emphasizes maintaining harmony between humans, ancestors, and the spirit world. Practices include temple offerings, festivals, and feng shui.

Hinduism

Originc. 20th century BC

Has no single founder; it developed over 4,000+ years in the Indian subcontinent from Vedic traditions, making it often considered the oldest living religion. Its texts include the Vedas, Upanishads, and Bhagavad Gita. Hindus may worship many deities seen as manifestations of one ultimate reality (Brahman). Key concepts are dharma (duty), karma (action and consequence), samsara (cycle of rebirth), and moksha (liberation).

Judaism

Originc. 20th century BC

Traces its origin to Abraham's covenant with God around 2000 BC and the giving of the Torah to Moses at Sinai. It shaped both Christianity and Islam. After the Second Temple's destruction (70 AD), rabbinic Judaism developed around study of the Torah and Talmud. Core beliefs are one God, a covenant with the Jewish people, and observance of commandments (mitzvot). Main modern branches are Orthodox, Conservative, and Reform.

Zoroastrianism

Originc. 15th – 10th century BC

Founded by the prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra) in ancient Persia, roughly 1500 to 1000 BC. It was the state religion of several Persian empires. It teaches belief in one supreme God, Ahura Mazda, and a cosmic struggle between good and evil. Humans choose through "good thoughts, good words, good deeds." It influenced Judaism, Christianity, and Islam; today's followers include the Parsis of India.

Jainism

Origin6th century BC

Shaped in India around the 6th century BC by Mahavira, the last of 24 Tirthankaras (spiritual teachers). Its central principle is ahimsa, absolute non-violence toward all living beings. Jains also practice truthfulness, non-attachment, and asceticism. The goal is freeing the soul from karma to achieve liberation.

Buddhism

Origin5th century BC

Founded in northern India around the 5th century BC by Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha ("awakened one"). It spread across Asia, developing into Theravada (Southeast Asia), Mahayana (East Asia), and Vajrayana (Tibet). It teaches the Four Noble Truths: life involves suffering, suffering comes from craving, it can end, and the Eightfold Path leads there. The goal is nirvana, release from the cycle of rebirth.

Christianity

Origin1st century AD

Originated in 1st-century Judea from the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth, whom followers believe is the Son of God and the Messiah. It spread across the Roman Empire and became its official religion in 380 AD. The Great Schism (1054) split it into Catholic and Orthodox branches, and the Reformation (16th century) created Protestantism. Core beliefs include the Trinity, salvation through Christ's death and resurrection, and the Bible as scripture.

Islam

Origin7th century AD

Founded in 7th-century Arabia by the Prophet Muhammad, who Muslims believe received God's final revelation, recorded in the Quran. It expanded rapidly across the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia. A succession dispute after Muhammad's death split it into Sunni and Shia branches. Core beliefs are strict monotheism (Allah) and the Five Pillars: faith, prayer, charity, fasting during Ramadan, and pilgrimage to Mecca.

Sikhism

Origin15th century AD

Founded in 15th-century Punjab by Guru Nanak and developed by nine successive Gurus. The final Guru declared the scripture, Guru Granth Sahib, the eternal Guru. Sikhs believe in one formless God, equality of all people, honest work, sharing with others, and constant remembrance of God. Baptized Sikhs observe the Five Ks, including uncut hair (kesh) and the steel bracelet (kara).

Bahá'í Faith

Origin19th century AD

Founded in 19th-century Persia by Bahá'u'lláh, preceded by the Báb. It teaches that all major religions come from the same God and represent stages of one unfolding revelation. Key principles include the unity of humanity, equality of men and women, harmony of science and religion, and universal education. It has no clergy and is administered by elected councils.

Non-Religious

Origin20th – 21st century AD

Includes atheists (no belief in gods), agnostics (uncertain whether gods exist), and the secular or unaffiliated. The group grew sharply in the 20th and 21st centuries, especially in Europe, East Asia, and North America. Members often draw on science, reason, and humanist ethics for meaning and morality.

Major Philosophical Traditions

8 schools of thought that shaped ethics, society, and ways of living, with or without belief in the divine, in chronological order.

Taoism

Origin6th century BC

Attributed to Laozi (6th century BC) and his text, the Tao Te Ching, with later development by Zhuangzi. It exists both as a philosophy and an organized religion. It teaches living in harmony with the Tao, the natural flow of the universe. Key ideas include wu wei (effortless action), simplicity, and the balance of yin and yang.

Confucianism

Origin6th – 5th century BC

Based on the teachings of Confucius (551 to 479 BC) in China, recorded in the Analects. It became the state ideology of imperial China for over 2,000 years. It stresses moral character, respect for family and elders (filial piety), proper social roles, and ethical leadership. The aim is a harmonious society built on virtue rather than force.

Greek Classical Philosophy

Origin5th – 4th century BC

Flourished in Athens in the 5th and 4th centuries BC. Socrates developed questioning to examine ethics, Plato proposed the theory of ideal Forms, and Aristotle systematized logic, science, ethics, and politics. Their work laid the foundation of Western philosophy, science, and political thought.

Stoicism

Originc. 3rd century BC

Founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, later practiced by Romans such as Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius. It teaches that virtue is the only true good. Stoics focus on what they can control (their judgments and actions) and accept what they cannot. It strongly influenced modern cognitive behavioral therapy.

Zen

Origin6th century AD

Developed in China as Chan Buddhism around the 6th century AD, influenced by Taoism, then spread to Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. It emphasizes meditation (zazen) and direct insight over scripture and doctrine. Teachers use koans, paradoxical questions, to break conventional thinking. The goal is sudden awakening to one's true nature.

Humanism

Origin14th – 16th century AD

Rooted in Renaissance thinkers who revived classical learning and focused on human potential. Modern secular humanism developed in the 19th and 20th centuries. It holds that people can lead ethical, meaningful lives through reason, science, and compassion without belief in the supernatural. It emphasizes human dignity, rights, and well-being.

Utilitarianism

Origin18th – 19th century AD

Developed in 18th and 19th-century England by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill. It judges actions by their consequences: the right action produces the greatest good for the greatest number. It shaped modern economics, public policy, and law. Critics argue it can justify harming individuals for the majority's benefit.

Existentialism

Origin19th century AD

Emerged in the 19th century with Kierkegaard and Nietzsche and peaked in mid-20th-century France with Sartre, de Beauvoir, and Camus. It holds that existence precedes essence: humans are not born with a fixed purpose but create meaning through choices. It emphasizes freedom, personal responsibility, and authenticity in a world without inherent meaning.

Theism & Views on God

6 positions on the existence and nature of God, from belief in a personal creator to atheism and agnosticism, in chronological order.

Theism (Classical / Monotheistic)

OriginAncient

Views God as a personal creator who is ontologically distinct from the universe (transcendent) yet present in and sustaining it (immanent). God acts in the world through will, providence, and revelation. Examples include Christianity, Judaism, and Islam, with key thinkers such as Augustine, Aquinas, Maimonides, and Avicenna.

Pantheism

Origin17th century AD

Asserts that God and the universe are identical. There is no separation: the divine is the totality of nature, its laws, and its substance. God is generally impersonal, with no will or intervention. Spinoza (Ethics, 1677) is the primary example. John Toland coined the term in 1705.

Deism

Origin18th century AD

God created the universe and its laws but does not intervene afterward. There is no revelation or miracles. Examples are Voltaire and several US founders, such as Jefferson and Paine.

Atheism

Origin18th century AD

The absence of belief in gods; it concerns belief, not knowledge. Weak atheism lacks belief in gods, while strong atheism asserts none exist. Its main arguments are lack of evidence, the problem of evil, and naturalism. Key figures are Holbach (1770), Russell, and Dawkins.

Panentheism

Origin19th century AD

Holds that God encompasses the entire universe but also transcends it. The universe is "in" God, but God is greater than the universe. It acts as a bridge between theism and pantheism: the divine is the underlying essence of all existence but is not limited to the material world. Karl Krause coined the term in 1828. Examples include process theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne) and strands of Hindu Vedanta.

Agnosticism

Origin19th century AD

Holds that God's existence is unknown or unknowable; it concerns knowledge, not belief. Strong agnosticism says it is unknowable in principle, and weak agnosticism says it is currently unknown. Huxley coined the term in 1869, building on Hume and Kant.
